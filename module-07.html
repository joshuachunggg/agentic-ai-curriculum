Topic 01

Where the Real Information Lives

Most practitioners consume too much low-signal content and not enough high-signal content. This is not because good sources are hard to find. It is because low-signal content is optimized for reach and low-signal content spreads faster. You need a deliberate information diet.

Here is how we rank sources, from highest to lowest signal quality for practitioners building agentic systems:

01
Primary sources: model and lab release notes
Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta AI — read the actual system cards and technical reports, not the blog summaries. Technical reports contain capability details and limitation admissions that never make it into press releases. This is where you learn what a model actually can and cannot do.
02
Working practitioners on X/Twitter
People building with these tools daily surface real limitations faster than anyone. They share what breaks, what surprises them, what changed their workflow. High signal but noisy — follow selectively. Key accounts as of 2026: Andrej Karpathy, Simon Willison, swyx, Hamel Husain, Jason Liu, Shreya Shankar, Harrison Chase, Brandon Hancock.
03
Conference talks — AI Engineer Summit, NeurIPS, ICLR
AI Engineer Summit is the best practitioner-focused conference for people building AI products. NeurIPS and ICLR are for research. Watch talks, not just read abstracts. Talks contain the reasoning behind decisions; abstracts contain only the conclusion.
04
Vendor and framework blogs
LangChain blog, Anthropic Engineering Blog, OpenAI research blog. Good for announcements, inherently promotional. Read them, but cross-reference with practitioner reactions before drawing conclusions about real-world performance.
05
General tech media and LinkedIn
TechCrunch, Wired, LinkedIn. Useful for understanding business context and public perception. Do not use these to make technical decisions. LinkedIn is especially filtered toward positive framing — almost never the first place real discoveries surface.
How to read a paper quickly

Read the abstract and conclusion first. If both are interesting, read the experiments section. Read related work only if you need context. Skip the rest unless the specific finding requires it. Most papers contain one key finding. Read for that finding, not for completeness.

Topic 02

Distinguishing Signal from Noise

The agentic AI space has a hype problem. Not because people are lying — most claims are technically defensible in the right narrow context. The problem is that demos are curated, benchmarks can be gamed, and terminology gets recycled to make old ideas sound new. You need pattern recognition for this.

Red Flag
Benchmark gaming
A model achieves SOTA on a benchmark but practitioners report it underperforms on real tasks. Ask: does this benchmark reflect your actual use case? Benchmarks are easy to saturate once labs optimize for them.
Red Flag
Rebadged concepts
"Agentic AI" was "AI assistants" was "intelligent agents" was "expert systems." When a new term explodes, ask: what is actually new here versus what is new branding on existing ideas?
Red Flag
Demo-to-production gap
Demos show the best case. Ask: what does this system do when input is ambiguous, incomplete, or adversarial? If you cannot get a clear answer, assume the gap is large.
Red Flag
Capability claims without evals
"Our model is 40% better" — at what, measured how, compared to what baseline, on what distribution of inputs? Claims without specific eval methodology are not evidence.
Signal
Independent practitioner agreement
When multiple practitioners running production systems independently report the same improvement without coordinating, that is a strong signal. One report is anecdote. Five independent reports are evidence.
Signal
Honest failure mode disclosure
When a lab or practitioner names the limitations alongside the wins, the wins are more credible. Honest failure disclosure is a positive signal, not a negative one.
Signal
Replicable in your environment
The most reliable signal: you ran it yourself on your eval set and it beat your baseline. Nothing substitutes for this. Build the eval infrastructure to make this fast.
Signal
Multiple independent evals
A capability that shows up across multiple independent benchmarks — not just the lab's own — with consistent results. Cross-source validation is hard to fake.
The hype cycle pattern

Every 6–12 months, a new paradigm is announced as "the end of X" or "the beginning of AGI." Track which predictions were accurate 12 months later. Most are not. This is not because the field is dishonest — it is because genuine capability improvements exist but the timeline and magnitude of real-world impact are consistently overestimated. Build institutional memory by noting predictions with dates.

Topic 03

Emerging Paradigms to Watch in 2026–2027

Several directions are moving from research into early production. We are not saying these will all arrive on schedule — predictions about AI timelines are historically overconfident. We are saying these are the directions where the research is credible and the production use cases are beginning to emerge.

Long-Horizon Planning
Current agents struggle with tasks that span days or weeks. Research direction: agents that set sub-goals, track progress across sessions, and recover from failures over long horizons. The unsolved problems are persistent state management, goal drift, and failure recovery that doesn't require starting from scratch. This is the limiting factor for truly autonomous knowledge work agents.
Watch
Agent Self-Improvement
Agents that evaluate their own outputs and iteratively improve them. Limited forms of this exist today — reflection loops, self-critique prompting. The research direction extends to agents that fine-tune themselves on their own successful trajectories. Concern: reward hacking and goal misalignment at scale. This is the direction that carries the most both promise and risk.
Emerging
Neuro-Symbolic Hybrids
Combining LLMs with symbolic reasoning systems — logic engines, formal verifiers. Useful for domains requiring guaranteed correctness: legal reasoning, mathematical proofs, safety-critical code. Mostly research in 2026, but watch for production deployments in regulated industries where correctness is non-negotiable and "usually right" is not enough.
Emerging
Multimodal Agents
Agents that natively perceive and act on images, audio, and video — not just text. Frontier models already have multimodal capabilities; the infrastructure for reliable multimodal tool use in agents is maturing fast. Near-term applications: agents that can see UIs, interpret charts, generate and edit media. The main gap is reliable structured output from visual inputs.
Maturing
AI-Native Frontier Firms
Organizations where agents handle the majority of knowledge work production — not assisting humans but operating as the primary production workforce, with humans in oversight and strategy roles. Already happening in narrow domains: coding, research summarization, customer support. The organizational design question: how do you manage, evaluate, and govern an agent workforce at scale?
Watch
Using Aria and COMPASS as benchmarks

You have now built or studied two concrete systems: Aria (a production customer success agent) and COMPASS (a 3-agent research pipeline). Use these as evaluation benchmarks when you encounter new frameworks, paradigms, or capability claims. Ask: would this capability meaningfully improve Aria's task completion rate or COMPASS's research quality? If the answer is unclear, the capability is probably not mature enough for adoption.

Topic 04

The Agent Factory Vision and Knowledge Work

The "agent factory" pattern — entire workflows run by coordinated agent pipelines with humans in oversight roles — is moving from architectural thought experiment to organizational reality. We covered the technical architecture in Module 4. Here we address the organizational implications.

Now — 12 mo
Agents accelerate individual contributors
A researcher with a COMPASS-style system produces 3–5x more synthesized output per day. A developer with a coding agent reviews more PRs and writes more tests. The primary organizational change is productivity multipliers for existing roles — not role elimination. The people who learn to work with agents effectively pull ahead of those who don't.
2–3 years
Team structures begin shifting
Teams need fewer people doing execution work and more people who can specify, evaluate, and govern agent systems. The skills that become more valuable: eval design, observability, prompt engineering at scale, agent orchestration architecture. The skills that become less valuable: rote data processing, templated content production, routine research synthesis.
Strategic
Durable advantage comes from institutional knowledge
The bottleneck will not be access to models — frontier models are widely available. The bottleneck will be knowing how to run agents reliably at scale. Teams that invest now in eval infrastructure, observability, and agent governance build compounding institutional knowledge. That knowledge is harder to replicate than model access.
The honest caveat on predictions

Predictions about AI timelines are historically overconfident. The near-term productivity claims in this section are grounded in current production deployments we can observe. The medium-term predictions carry significant uncertainty. Build for optionality — systems that can absorb new capabilities without being rebuilt from scratch — rather than betting on a specific future timeline.

Topic 05

Evaluating New Models and Capabilities Quickly

New model releases happen frequently. You cannot do a full evaluation of every one. But you also cannot rely on published benchmarks alone — they measure the lab's priorities, not yours. You need a fast, repeatable evaluation protocol that gives you an honest answer about whether a new model or capability is worth deeper integration.

01
Take 20–30 representative inputs from your actual use case
Not cherry-picked successes. Draw from your existing eval set — the same inputs you used to validate your current system. If you don't have an eval set, this is the first thing to build.
02
Run the new model or capability on all inputs
Same prompts, same tool definitions, same context structure. Control everything except the variable you are testing. If you change multiple things, you won't know what caused any difference.
03
Score against your existing eval criteria
Task completion rate, faithfulness to source material, cost per call, latency. Use the same rubric you used to evaluate your current system. If your criteria have changed, re-run your baseline first.
04
Compare to your current baseline
Is the new model better, worse, or roughly equal on your specific inputs? By how much? A 3% improvement that costs 40% more is not an upgrade. A 15% improvement in task completion on your hardest cases almost certainly is.
05
Catalog failure modes explicitly
What categories of inputs does the new model struggle with that your current model handles? New models introduce new failure modes even while fixing old ones. Name them before adopting.
06
Decide on integration
Only then decide if the new model or capability is worth deeper integration. The question is not "is this impressive?" but "does this improve my specific agent's performance on my eval set?"
Red flags in model releases

Safety evaluations only on curated prompt sets. Benchmark results without confidence intervals. No mention of failure modes or regressions from the previous version. No independent replication of key capability claims. Any of these should make you cautious about the results — not dismissive, but cautious. Run your own eval before drawing conclusions.

Topic 06

Building a Learning System for Your Team

Individual learning is necessary but not sufficient. One person staying current creates a single point of knowledge failure. You need a shared learning system — a set of practices that distributes frontier awareness across the team and converts raw information into institutional knowledge.

What works
  • Weekly frontier sync — 20 minutes, one person brings one finding, group evaluates signal vs. noise
  • Shared experiment log — record attempts and failures, not just successes
  • "Build it to understand it" — 2–4 hour budget for frontier exploration before committing to evaluation
  • Eval-first adoption — any new capability must pass your eval harness before production consideration
  • Quarterly 30-minute internal talks — one person presents what they learned, forces synthesis
What doesn't work
  • Reading newsletters without building anything — content without context doesn't transfer
  • Tracking every model release without a framework for evaluation — you'll be perpetually reactive
  • Assigning frontier awareness to one person — creates a knowledge silo that leaves when they do
  • Evaluating in weeks what you could test in hours — prototypes are faster than debates
  • Only sharing what worked — failure logs are more valuable than success stories
The experiment log

A shared document where anyone on the team can record: "We tried X. Here's what we expected. Here's what happened. Here's what we think it means." Failures are especially valuable — they prevent the team from re-running the same experiment six months later. The discipline of writing it down also forces the author to think through what they actually learned.

Ongoing Practice

The Weekly Frontier Sync

Knowledge that stays with one person decays the moment that person leaves, gets busy, or shifts focus. The weekly frontier sync converts individual learning into team capability. It is the simplest high-leverage practice we know for staying current as a team.

Format

Weekly 20-Minute Frontier Sync

One person brings a new capability, paper, or practitioner insight they encountered that week. The group evaluates together: is this signal or noise? How would it change what we are building?

  • 0–2 min Presenter gives context: where did you find this, why did it catch your attention?
  • 2–8 min Presenter explains the finding: what does it claim, what is the evidence, what did you observe if you tested it?
  • 8–15 min Group evaluation: apply the signal/noise framework from Topic 02. Is the claim independently verifiable? Does it reflect an actual use case we care about?
  • 15–18 min Action One of three outcomes: (1) log it in the experiment queue, (2) log it as watch-and-wait, or (3) dismiss with a note on why.
  • 18–20 min Rotate — who brings next week's finding?

Facilitation tips

Rotate the presenter role. If one person always brings findings, the team gets one person's filtered view of the frontier. The rotation forces everyone to stay in the information stream.

Log the outcomes, not just the meetings. A shared doc with "this week's finding" and a one-sentence verdict — signal, noise, or watch — becomes a searchable institutional record. Six months later, you will know which predictions held up.

Keep the format strict. Twenty minutes is enough for one finding. If a finding generates so much discussion that you overrun the time, that is itself a signal — log it for follow-up outside the sync.

Why this works

The discipline of presenting a finding to a group — and having that group ask hard questions — forces you to evaluate what you actually understand about it. Most noise is eliminated by the simple act of having to explain it out loud to skeptical peers. The signal survives that scrutiny. The noise doesn't.

Module 07 Lab

Design Aria's Eval Protocol

Before you write a single line of capstone code, define what "working correctly" means. An eval written after the system is built tends to pass because it was written to match what was built — not to test whether what was built is correct. This lab breaks that pattern.

The capstone (Module 08) asks you to build and run an eval suite for Aria. This lab is the design phase that comes first. You'll define the dimensions, write the test cases, assign the weights, and score a hypothetical response by hand — all before touching any code. The goal is a written spec you can hand to a colleague who would know, unambiguously, whether a given Aria response passes or fails.

Step 01
Define five dimensions of correctness
Write five dimensions that together capture whether Aria is working correctly. Each dimension should be specific enough that two evaluators looking at the same response would agree on a score. "Responds helpfully" fails this test. "Issues refund and sends confirmation email for eligible delayed orders" passes. Draft all five before assigning any weights — sequence matters less than coverage. Ask yourself: what failure would each dimension catch that the others wouldn't?
Step 02
Write ten test cases
For each test case, write: the input (what the customer says), the expected behavior (what Aria should do — specific actions, not vague goals), and the failure signal (what would tell you Aria got it wrong). Cover: a normal small refund, a high-value escalation, a happy-path non-issue, a prompt injection attempt, an unknown order ID, and at least four cases of your own that reflect real failure modes you've seen or can imagine. Do not write test cases designed to pass easily.
Step 03
Assign point weights
Distribute 100 points across your five dimensions. The weights encode your priorities: which failures matter most in production? A system that resolves correctly 90% of the time but occasionally violates policy is worse than one that resolves correctly 80% of the time and never violates policy — your weights should reflect that. Write one sentence per dimension explaining why it deserves its weight. If you can't justify the weight, reconsider it.
Step 04
Score a hypothetical response by hand
Apply your rubric to this response: "Hi Alex, I looked up your order ORD-001 and I can see it's been delayed by 14 days. Per our policy, I've issued a full refund of $89.99 to your original payment method. You'll receive a confirmation email within a few minutes. I'm sorry for the inconvenience — is there anything else I can help with?" Score it against each dimension. Where is the response strong? Where does it lose points? Now change the scenario: what if the order was $649 instead of $89.99 — does the same response pass or fail? Why?
Step 05
Compare to the capstone eval
After completing Module 08 Phase 5, open eval.py and compare its rubric and test cases to yours. Where do they overlap? Where do they diverge? Which test cases did you write that the capstone's eval misses? Which did the capstone's eval catch that yours missed? The gaps in both directions are the most informative part: they tell you where your mental model of Aria's risk profile differs from the one encoded in the course material — and who's right is worth debating.
Capstone Connection

The eval.py in Module 08 Phase 5 implements a rubric covering five dimensions (resolve correctly, stay in policy, escalate appropriately, handle errors cleanly, communicate clearly) with eight test cases. The test cases you write here become the custom test cases you add in Phase 5 Step 03. If you skip this exercise and go straight to coding, you'll end up with an eval that passes because you wrote it to match what you built — not because Aria actually works. Define the spec first. Build to it second. Measure against it third.

Checkpoint

Before moving to the capstone: you should have a written rubric (5 dimensions, point weights summing to 100), 10 test cases each with input, expected behavior, and failure signal, and a manually scored sample response. If any dimension is vague enough that two evaluators might score it differently, sharpen it. Vague specs produce passing evals that miss real failures.

Completing This Curriculum, You Can Now...

[01] Design and reason about the autonomy spectrum — from simple tool-augmented LLMs through fully autonomous multi-agent pipelines — and make deliberate placement decisions based on risk and reversibility.
[02] Build reliable ReAct agents with proper tool design, memory architecture, and planning loops. Apply the 12-Factor Agent framework to make systems production-ready.
[03] Engineer context windows deliberately — manage what goes in, when it goes in, and how it is structured for agent use. Apply RAG patterns that match your latency and accuracy requirements.
[04] Architect multi-agent systems using orchestrator/subagent patterns. Implement MCP servers for tool sharing and A2A protocols for cross-agent communication.
[05] Deploy agents safely — design guardrails, implement HITL checkpoints calibrated to action risk, apply EU AI Act compliance thinking, and defend against prompt injection and other agent-specific attacks.
[06] Build and run evals. Know the difference between unit evals, integration evals, and human evals. Use observability tooling to trace failures and improve systems based on real production data.
[07] Stay current. Apply the signal/noise framework to new model releases and capability claims. Run your own evaluation protocol before adopting anything new. Build team learning systems that convert individual knowledge into shared institutional capability.

What "Frontier" Actually Means

Frontier does not mean knowing everything. No one knows everything in this field, including the people building the models. Frontier means having the tools and habits to learn continuously — to read new information with calibrated skepticism, to test claims against your own systems, and to update your understanding without getting whiplashed by every new announcement.

The practitioners who stay dangerous in this field are not the ones who know the most right now. They are the ones who have built reliable systems for knowing what to pay attention to. They have an information diet, an evaluation protocol, and a team culture that treats staying current as infrastructure rather than optional reading.

You have spent six modules building the technical foundations. This final module is the operating system that runs underneath all of it. The frameworks will change. The models will improve. The vocabulary will shift. The underlying discipline — building carefully, evaluating honestly, learning continuously — does not.

That is what you carry forward from here.
